Information Systems in Global Business Today

Lecture 01 · Why IS matters, definitions, dimensions & strategic objectives

What is an Information System?

An information system is a set of interrelated components that collect, process, store and distribute information to support decision making and control in an organization. In addition to supporting decision making, coordination and control, information systems may also help managers and workers analyze problems, visualize complex subjects, and create new products. This definition is highly examinable — remember the four verbs (collect, process, store, distribute) and the purpose (decision making & control).

Information System = interrelated components → collect · process · store · distribute information → support decision making & control

Data vs Information

Data are raw facts representing events in the organization before they have been organized into a useful form, while information is data that has been shaped into a meaningful and useful context. The classic example from the lectures: raw data from a supermarket checkout counter can be processed and organized to produce meaningful information, such as the total unit sales of dish detergent or the total sales revenue from dish detergent for a specific store. In other words, information is data made useful through processing, organization and context.

Functions of an Information System

An information system contains information about an organization and its surrounding environment. Three activities in an information system produce the information that organizations need to make decisions, control operations, analyze problems, and create new products or services. These activities are summarized in the flow below — feedback is the output that is returned to appropriate members of the organization to help them evaluate or correct the input stage.

Environmental actors such as customers, suppliers, competitors, stockholders, and regulatory agencies constantly interact with the organization and its information systems. A good illustrative example is Disney World's system for controlling crowds: sensors capture crowd data (input), the system processes it, and outputs guide staff to redirect crowds — while feedback refines future decisions.

✏️ Worked example — spot the IS activities

A supermarket payroll system captures each employee's hourly attendance (1), calculates gross pay, taxes and net pay (2), and prints payslips for employees (3). Employees complain that overtime hours were under-recorded, so HR corrects the timesheet data (4). Identify activities 1–4.

Solution
  • 1 = Input — capturing raw attendance/time data.
  • 2 = Processing — converting raw data into pay calculations.
  • 3 = Output — distributing processed information (payslips).
  • 4 = Feedback — output (wrong pay) returned to correct the input stage.

The Three Dimensions of Information Systems

There is a fundamental interdependence between organizations and information systems: changes in strategy, rules and business processes increasingly require changes in hardware, software, databases and telecommunications — and often, what an organization would like to do depends on what its systems will permit it to do. To understand an information system you must study all three dimensions: organization, management and technology. Exam questions frequently ask which dimension a given problem belongs to.

1 · Organization
Key elements: people, structure, business processes, politics, and culture. IS are integral to organizations — for some firms (e.g. credit reporting firms) there would be no business without an IS.
2 · Management
Managers perceive challenges, set strategy, allocate resources and coordinate work. Authority is a hierarchy / pyramid: senior → middle → operational management.
3 · Technology
Hardware (physical equipment), software (preprogrammed instructions), data management technology (organizing data on storage media), networking & telecom (linking hardware, moving data).

What's New in MIS?

Five broad changes are making information systems more relevant than ever: IT innovations (e.g. cloud computing such as Google Drive and OneDrive), new business models (e.g. Netflix), the expansion of e-commerce, management changes, and changes in firms and organizations themselves. Together these shifts explain why MIS is no longer a back-office concern but a core business discipline. Firms that ignore them risk being disrupted by competitors that use technology more aggressively.

How IS are Transforming Business & Globalization

Information systems are transforming business through increased technology investments, providing economic value to business, increased responsiveness to customer demands, shifts in media, advertising and commerce, and new laws governing information systems. The mobile phone industry is the classic illustration — an industry that barely existed decades ago now connects billions of customers worldwide and reshapes banking, retail and entertainment.

Globalization and IS go hand in hand. Consider these facts from the lecture: about 80% of the toys sold in the U.S. are manufactured in China, while about 90% of the PCs manufactured in China use American-made Intel or AMD chips. Not just goods move across borders — so do jobs. The 24×7 culture, the Internet's reduction of the cost of operating on a global scale, and the emergence of technology-based companies such as Google, eBay and Amazon.com all show how deeply information systems enable globalization.

The emergence of the digital firm

A digital firm is one in which nearly all of the organization's significant business relationships with customers, suppliers, and employees are digitally enabled and mediated. In a true digital firm, any piece of information required to support key business decisions is available at any time and anywhere in the firm. While very few firms are fully digital, most are moving toward this ideal — making digital integration a strategic goal rather than a technical afterthought.

Contemporary Approaches to Information Systems

Information systems can be studied from two complementary viewpoints. The technical approach treats IS as mathematically based models and technologies, while the behavioral approach deals with behavioral issues that arise in the development and long-term maintenance of information systems. Neither alone is sufficient — real-world systems need both.

ApproachContributing disciplinesFocus
TechnicalComputer scienceTheories of computability, methods of computation, efficient data storage & access
Management scienceDevelopment of models for decision-making and management practices
Operations researchMathematical techniques for optimizing parameters — transportation, inventory control, transaction costs
BehavioralPsychology, sociology, economics (behavioral issues)Strategic business integration, design, implementation, utilization and management — issues that cannot be usefully explored with technical models alone
Common confusion The behavioral approach exists because issues like strategic business integration, design, implementation, utilization and management of systems cannot be explored usefully with the models used in the technical approach. If an exam question mentions "resistance to change" or "organizational politics" — that is behavioral, not technical.

Global E-Business: Types of Information Systems

Lecture 02 · Business processes, TPS · MIS · DSS · ESS, enterprise applications, e-business

Business Processes and Information Systems

Business processes are sets of activities — the steps required to produce a product or a service. They are the workflows of material, information and knowledge through the organization. Processes may be tied to a single functional area (e.g. Human Resources hiring employees) or be cross-functional, spanning several areas (e.g. fulfilling a customer order, which involves sales, warehousing, production and accounting). Business processes are so central that a business can be seen as a collection of business processes — and processes may be assets or liabilities depending on how well they are designed.

Functional areaExample business processes
Manufacturing & productionAssembling the product · checking for quality · producing bills of materials
Sales & marketingIdentifying customers · making customers aware of the product · selling the product
Finance & accountingPaying creditors · creating financial statements · managing cash accounts
Human resourcesHiring employees · evaluating job performance · enrolling employees in benefits plans

How IT enhances business processes

Information technology enhances business processes in two main ways. First, it increases the efficiency of existing processes by automating steps that were previously manual. Second, it enables entirely new processes capable of transforming the business — changing the flow of information, replacing sequential steps with parallel steps, eliminating delays in decision making, and supporting new business models. Examples: the Kindle e-book from Amazon (a whole new digital delivery model) and buying a computer online at Best Buy (an existing process transformed by a new channel).

Types of Information Systems

The lecture organizes systems into two big families: systems for different management groups (TPS at the bottom, business-intelligence systems for middle and senior management) and systems for linking the enterprise (enterprise applications, intranets and extranets). Learn the ladder below — it is one of the most examined diagrams in the whole course.

1 · Transaction Processing Systems (TPS)

TPS perform and record the daily routine transactions necessary to conduct business — such as sales order entry, payroll, shipping. They allow managers to monitor the status of internal operations and relations with the external environment, they serve the operational levels of the organization, and they handle predefined, structured goals and decision making. A payroll TPS keeps track of money paid to employees; because TPS record every transaction, they are the major source of data for all other systems. TPS failures can stop the whole business — imagine a stopped airline reservation system — so they must be reliable and fast.

2 · Systems for Business Intelligence (BI)

Business intelligence is a class of software applications that analyze current and historical data to find patterns and trends and aid decision-making. BI systems are used to support middle and senior management: for middle management they support monitoring, controlling, decision-making and administrative activities; for senior management they support more non-routine decision making. BI turns the flood of transaction data into insight.

Functional business-intelligence systems

Beyond the classic MIS, the lecture lists five specialized management-support systems. A Marketing Information System helps managers identify key customers and perform market segmentation, leading to accurate identification of the target market. A Human Resource Information System (HRIS) allows HR managers to take better and timely decisions, with modules for payroll, time & attendance, benefits administration, HR management, training, and employee self-service. A Finance Management Information System (FMIS) helps managers evaluate sources of funds — which suit the firm best, bear the least risk, and reap the most gain (e.g. choosing between equity and debt). A Production Management Information System (PMIS) is concerned with planning and control of all activities that transform inputs into outputs by adding value — production planning, production control, product design & development, and plant location & layout.

Relationships among systems — the constituency perspective

Viewed from the constituency perspective: TPS support operational-level employees; MIS and DSS support managers; ESS support executives. The systems are also related to one another: TPS are the major source of data for other systems, while ESS are recipients of data from lower-level systems. Data may be exchanged between systems, although in reality most businesses' systems are only loosely integrated — but they are getting better.

✏️ Worked example — MIS obtains its data from TPS

Three TPS (sales order, payroll, shipping) supply summarized transaction data to the MIS reporting system at the end of each period. A middle manager asks: "Which product line had the highest sales revenue this quarter?" Why is this an MIS question, and where does the answer come from?

Solution

It is a routine question with a predefined procedure for answering — summarize the period's transaction data into a report. The raw data originates in the TPS, which records every sales transaction; the MIS summarizes that data and delivers the report (e.g. summarized annual sales data). No external data or "what-if" modelling is needed — if it were (e.g. "what if prices drop 10%?"), it would be a DSS question.

Systems for Linking the Enterprise

Enterprise applications are systems for linking the enterprise. They span functional areas, execute business processes across the firm, and include all levels of management. Their architecture automates processes that span multiple business functions and organizational levels and may extend outside the organization. There are four major enterprise applications, summarized below (each is covered in depth in Topics 5–7).

Intranets and Extranets

Two alternative tools increase integration and expedite the flow of information. Intranets are internal company web sites accessible only by employees. Extranets are company web sites accessible externally only to vendors and suppliers, and are often used to coordinate the supply chain. Both are cheaper, lighter-weight alternatives to full enterprise applications for linking the enterprise.

Using IS to Achieve Competitive Advantage

Lecture 03 · Organizations, Porter's Five Forces, Value Chain Analysis

What is an Organization?

An organization can be defined in two complementary ways. The technical definition: an organization is a stable, formal social structure that takes resources from the environment and processes them to produce outputs — a formal legal entity with internal rules and procedures, as well as a social structure. The behavioral definition is more realistic: an organization is a collection of rights, privileges, obligations, and responsibilities that is delicately balanced over a period of time through conflict and conflict resolution. The technical view sees structure; the behavioral view sees people and politics.

Technical definition
Stable + formal social structure + takes resources from environment + processes them to produce outputs
Behavioral definition
Collection of rights, privileges, obligations, responsibilities — balanced over time through conflict and conflict resolution

Features of organizations

  • Use of hierarchical structure — levels of authority stacked in a pyramid.
  • Accountability, authority in a system of impartial decision making.
  • Adherence to the principle of efficiency — firms seek to maximize output from given inputs.
  • Routines and business processes — the operational DNA of the firm.
  • Organizational politics, culture, environments and structures.

Routines (also called Standard Operating Procedures — SOPs) are precise rules, procedures and practices developed to cope with virtually all expected situations. Business processes are collections of routines, and a business firm is a collection of business processes. New information system applications require that individual routines and business processes change to achieve high levels of organizational performance — which explains why systems projects so often disturb people.

Politics, culture and environments

Organizational politics arise because divergent viewpoints lead to political struggle, competition and conflict — and political resistance greatly hampers organizational change, including new information systems. Organizational culture encompasses the bedrock, unassailable, unquestioned assumptions that define the organization's goals and products — what products the organization should produce, how, where and for whom. Organizational environments have a reciprocal relationship with the organization: firms are open to and dependent on the social and physical environment, can influence their environments, and environments generally change faster than organizations. Information systems can be an instrument of environmental scanning, acting as a lens on the outside world.

Disruptive technologies

A disruptive technology brings about sweeping change to businesses, industries and markets. The lecture highlights first movers — the inventors of disruptive technologies — and fast followers — firms with the size and resources to capitalize on that technology. Classic examples: microprocessor chips (1971), personal computers (1975), digital photography using CCD image sensors (1975), the World Wide Web (1989), Internet music/video/TV services (1998), the PageRank algorithm (ranking web pages by popularity), and software as a web service (remote access to online software).

The Impact of IS on Organizations

Economically, IT changes the relative costs of capital and the costs of information — information systems technology is a factor of production, like capital and labor, and it affects the cost and quality of information. Because IT can reduce transaction costs (the cost of participating in markets), it helps firms contract in size while growing output — the economic logic behind outsourcing. Behaviorally, IT flattens organizations: decision making is pushed to lower levels and fewer managers are needed, because IT enables faster decisions and increases each manager's span of control. In postindustrial organizations, authority increasingly relies on knowledge and competence rather than formal positions.

Organizations also show strong resistance to change. Information systems become bound up in organizational politics because they influence access to a key resource — information — and they potentially change an organization's structure, culture, politics and work. The most common reason for failure of large projects is organizational and political resistance to change. The Internet increases the accessibility, storage and distribution of information and knowledge, dramatically lowers transaction and agency costs, and is now a key component of every firm's IT infrastructure. When designing systems, you must consider the environment, the organization's structure, culture and politics, the type of organization and leadership style, the interest groups affected, and the kinds of tasks, decisions and business processes the system will assist.

Exam tip — economic vs behavioral impacts Economics questions mention transaction costs, agency costs, factors of production, firm size, outsourcing. Behavioral questions mention flattening hierarchies, span of control, postindustrial authority, resistance to change, politics. Classify the clue words and you classify the answer.

Porter's Five Forces Model

Porter's Five Forces model analyzes the competitive forces acting on a firm and its industry. Each force comes with its own determinants — a very common exam format is to give a determinant (e.g. "switching costs") and ask which force it belongs to.

ForceDeterminants / barriers (memorize)
1 · Threat of new entrantsDepends on barriers to entry: economies of scale · product differentiation · capital investments · switching cost · government policy · patent right · brand preference / customer loyalty
2 · Rivalry among existing competitorsConcentration ratio · industry growth · relationship between competitors · competencies (resources, leadership, brand, innovation) · exit barriers (redundancy payments, effect of withdrawal on other operations, reluctance of management to accept defeat, government pressure)
3 · Threat of substitutesSatisfy the same need; depend on price · relative performance · switching cost · change of need/taste
4 · Bargaining power of customers/buyersProduct variety · product differentiation · pricing · income of buyers · buyers' knowledge of alternative products · volume purchased · price sensitivity of buyers
5 · Bargaining power of suppliersNumber of suppliers · % of suppliers' business to the company · market share / size of suppliers · importance of the supplier's product to the buyer

Information System Strategies for Dealing with Competitive Forces

1 · Low-cost leadership
Use IS to produce products & services at a lower price than competitors while enhancing quality and service
2 · Product differentiation
Use IS to differentiate products and enable new services & products
3 · Focus on market niche
Use IS to enable a focused strategy on a single market niche — specialize
4 · Customer & supplier intimacy
Use IS to develop strong ties and loyalty with customers and suppliers

Impact of the Internet on Competitive Forces

  • Substitute products/services — enables substitutes with new approaches to meeting needs and performing functions.
  • Customers' bargaining power — availability of global price and product information shifts bargaining power to customers.
  • Suppliers' bargaining power — procurement over the Internet tends to raise buyers' power over suppliers; but suppliers also benefit from reduced barriers to entry and elimination of distributors/intermediaries.
  • Threat of new entrants — the Internet reduces barriers to entry (no need for a sales force, channels, physical assets) and provides a technology for driving business processes.
  • Rivalry among competitors — widens the geographic market, increases the number of competitors, reduces differences among them, makes operational advantages harder to sustain, and puts pressure to compete on price.

Porter's Value Chain Analysis

Porter says "Competitive advantage arises out of the way in which firms organize themselves and perform their activities." Value Chain Analysis (VCA) analyzes the means by which a firm creates value in its products from the perspective of the end user; management should analyze their business in terms of how it generates value for customers and shareholders. The chain splits into five primary activities and four support activities. Learn every activity with its definition — this is one of the highest-frequency essay/MCQ items in the course.

Extending the value chain: the value web

A value web is a collection of independent firms that use information technology to coordinate their value chains to collectively produce a product or service for a market. It is more customer driven and operates in a less linear fashion than the traditional value chain. The value web is a networked system that can synchronize the value chains of business partners within an industry to respond rapidly to changes in supply and demand.

How IS Improves Business-Unit Performance

Three mechanisms stand out. Synergies: when the output of some units can be used as inputs to other units, or when two organizations pool their markets and expertise — merging with or acquiring related firms lowers costs and boosts value (e.g. merging with a supplier). Core competencies: activities where the firm is a world-class leader; IS encourage knowledge sharing across business units, expanding the competencies' reach. Network-based strategies: using network economics — the value of a product grows as more people use it (e.g. virtual companies, virtual communities); a firm with a large community of users gains a competitive edge.

Challenges of Strategic Information Systems

Strategic systems are powerful but hard to manage. Firms face three big challenges: sustaining competitive advantage (advantages erode as competitors copy them), aligning IT with business objectives (systems must serve strategy, not just technology fashion), and managing strategic transitions — the movement between the old system and the new technology and strategy, which often requires painful organizational change. These challenges explain why many strategic IS initiatives fail despite sound technology.

What is ethics?

Simply put, ethics is about professional behavior. Formally, ethics refers to the principles of right and wrong that individuals, acting as free moral agents, use to make choices to guide their behaviors. Social issues, by contrast, are matters of concern to governments and the community. When you act as a "free moral agent" you accept responsibility for the consequences of your choices — this is why the basic concepts below matter so much in court cases and professional practice.

Basic Concepts: Ethics in an Information Society

Five basic concepts form the vocabulary of information ethics — each has a precise meaning that exam questions test directly. Responsibility means accepting the potential costs, duties and obligations for the decisions you make. Accountability is a feature of systems and social institutions: it means determining who should take responsibility for decisions and actions — mechanisms exist to identify who did what and when. Liability extends responsibility further, legally placing responsibility with a person or group (so injured parties can be compensated). Due process is a related feature of societies where laws are applied fairly and correctly through known, established procedures. Finally, digital trust — transparency and data integrity — underpins every online relationship.

1 · Information rights: privacy

Privacy is the claim of individuals to be left alone, free from surveillance or interference. In practice, the lecture links privacy concerns to web site policies and cookies — small files stored by web sites on visitors' hard drives that can track their browsing. Users often have little idea of how their personal data is collected, aggregated and sold, which is why information rights sit at the top of the moral-dimensions list.

2 · Property rights: intellectual property

Intellectual property (IP) consists of tangible and intangible products of the mind created by individuals or corporations. Because digital content is trivially easy to copy, the information age challenges traditional IP protection. The key instruments are copyrights — a statutory grant protecting creators of intellectual property from having their work copied by others — and patents — a grant giving the owner an exclusive monopoly on the ideas behind an invention for 20 years. Many laws and court precedents on accountability, liability and control were strongly in place long before computers were invented. Along with privacy and property laws, new information technologies are challenging existing liability law and social practices for holding individuals and institutions accountable. As systems grow more automated and AI-driven, the chain of responsibility becomes harder to trace.

4 · System quality

We heavily depend on and trust information systems, so data quality issues become critically important — they affect both customers and users. The related problem is system errors: software bugs and errors that can produce harm at scale. The moral question is what standards of data and system quality society should demand to protect individual rights and safety.

5 · Quality of life

The fifth dimension asks what values should be preserved in an information- and knowledge-based society, which institutions should be protected from violation, and which cultural values and practices the new technology supports. Concerns range from dependency and dehumanization to the digital divide and the balance between work and life that "always-on" connectivity disturbs.

Emerging Concerns: AI Ethics, Data Governance, Algorithmic Bias

The lecture closes with three modern concerns that extend the classic framework. AI ethics asks how autonomous, learning systems can be kept fair, transparent and accountable when their decisions affect people's lives. Data governance concerns the policies and frameworks that determine who owns, controls and may use data — essential when data crosses borders and jurisdictions (a problem amplified by cloud and edge computing). Algorithmic bias arises when trained models systematically disadvantage groups of people; because AI/ML learns from historical data, biased inputs silently produce biased outputs — which is why transparency and auditing have become central to modern IS governance.

Enterprise Systems (ERP)

Enterprise systems — also called Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems — are a suite of integrated software modules and a common central database. They collect data from many divisions of the firm for use in nearly all of the firm's internal business activities, and information entered in one process is immediately available for other processes. ERP resolves the problem of fragmented, redundant data sets and systems by storing everything once, in one place.

ERP characteristic 1
Real-time operations — data is captured and made available instantly
ERP characteristic 2
Integration of all business activities — one suite across the firm
ERP characteristic 3
Flow of information — output of one process feeds the others immediately

Enterprise software and how ERP works

Enterprise software is built around thousands of predefined business processes that reflect best practices. The software supports processes across finance & accounting (general ledger, accounts payable), human resources (personnel administration, payroll), manufacturing & production (purchasing, shipping) and sales & marketing (order processing, billing, sales planning). To implement enterprise software, firms select the functions of the system they wish to use, map their business processes to the software processes, and customize using the software's configuration tables.

Business value of enterprise systems 1· Increase operational efficiency · 2· Provide firm-wide information to support decision making · 3· Enable rapid responses to customer requests for information or products · 4· Include analytical tools to evaluate overall organizational performance.

Supply Chain Management Systems

A supply chain is a network of organizations and business processes for procuring raw materials, transforming them into intermediate and finished products, and distributing the products to customers. Think of Nike's supply chain: materials from suppliers flow through factories and distributors to retail stores worldwide. The upstream supply chain includes the firm's suppliers, the suppliers' suppliers, and the processes for managing relationships with them. The downstream supply chain includes the organizations and processes responsible for distributing and delivering products to customers. Memory hook: suppliers are upstream (before your firm); customers are downstream (after it).

Information and supply chain management

Supply chain inefficiencies are enormous — they can waste as much as 25 percent of a company's operating costs. Three concepts describe how companies cope. Under a just-in-time (JIT) strategy, components arrive exactly when they are needed and finished goods are shipped as they leave the assembly line — minimizing inventory. Safety stock acts as a buffer for the lack of flexibility in the supply chain, protecting against uncertainty at the cost of holding inventory. The bullwhip effect occurs when information about product demand gets distorted as it passes from one entity to the next along the supply chain — small changes in customer demand amplify into wild swings in orders upstream, causing over- and under-stocking.

Exam favourite — bullwhip effect Definition to reproduce: "Information about product demand gets distorted as it passes from one entity to the next across the supply chain." It is caused by delayed, distorted information; the cure is immediate, shared information across supply chain partners — exactly what SCM systems provide.

How information systems facilitate supply chain management

  • Decide when and what to produce, store, and move
  • Rapidly communicate orders
  • Track the status of orders
  • Check inventory availability and monitor inventory levels
  • Reduce inventory, transportation and warehousing costs
  • Track shipments
  • Plan production based on actual customer demand
  • Rapidly communicate changes in product design

Supply chain management software

Supply chain planning systems model the existing supply chain and optimize sourcing and manufacturing plans. They establish inventory levels (raw, intermediate, finished), decide where to store finished products, identify transportation modes, and facilitate demand planning — matching supply forecasts with actual orders. Supply chain execution systems manage the flow of products through distribution centers and warehouses to ensure that products are delivered to the right locations in the most efficient manner. Planning = decide; execution = deliver.

Global supply chains

The Internet helps companies manage many aspects of global supply chains — sourcing, transportation, communications, international finance. But global chains bring their own issues: they span greater geographic distances and time differences; they create more complex pricing issues (local taxes, transportation); they must respect foreign government regulations; and they must bridge cultural differences. Globalization multiplies both the value of SCM systems and the difficulty of running them.

Business value of SCM systems

SCM systems deliver value by matching supply to demand: they reduce inventory levels, improve delivery service, speed product time to market, and use assets more effectively. Reduced supply chain costs lead to increased profitability, and better availability and service drive increased sales. The payoff compounds because supply chain improvements touch every product the firm sells.

The emerging Internet-driven supply chain

The emerging Internet-driven supply chain operates like a digital logistics nervous system. It provides multidirectional communication among firms, networks of firms and e-marketplaces so that entire networks of supply chain partners can immediately adjust inventories, orders and capacities. When demand shifts anywhere in the network, the whole chain can respond in real time — the practical realization of the concurrent model at global scale.

✏️ Worked example — diagnose the supply chain problem

A furniture retailer sees a 10% rise in demand for a sofa. Its distributor orders 30% more from the factory "to be safe"; the factory orders 50% more fabric. Three months later the chain is drowning in unsold stock. Name the effect, explain it, and recommend two IS-based remedies.

Solution

This is the bullwhip effect: information about product demand got distorted as it passed from one entity to the next — each layer exaggerated the order to protect itself (and held safety stock as a buffer for inflexibility).

Remedies: (1) A shared SCM system giving all partners visibility of actual customer demand (point-of-sale data) so production is planned on real orders — a pull-based approach; (2) Use supply chain planning software for demand planning, and execution systems to track inventory and shipments, reducing the need for each layer to over-order. JIT delivery scheduling would further cut buffer stock.

Customer Relationship Management Systems (CRM)

To build and nurture strong, long-lasting customer relationships, a firm needs to know exactly who its customers are, how to contact them, whether they are costly to service and sell to, what products and services they are interested in, and how much they spend with the company. In large businesses there are too many customers and too many ways for customers to interact with the firm to track this by hand. CRM systems solve the problem: they capture and integrate customer data from all over the organization, consolidate and analyze that data, distribute customer information to systems and customer touch points across the enterprise, and provide a single enterprise view of customers. CRM systems examine customers from a multifaceted perspective, using a set of integrated applications to address all aspects of the customer relationship — customer service, sales and marketing.

CRM software: modules and tools

CRM packages range from niche tools to large-scale enterprise applications. The more comprehensive packages include two major modules. Partner Relationship Management (PRM) enhances collaboration between a company and its selling partners: it provides the ability to trade information, distribute leads and customer data, and integrate lead generation, pricing, promotions, order configurations and availability; it also provides tools to assess partners' performances. Employee Relationship Management (ERM) deals with employee issues closely related to CRM — setting objectives, employee performance management, performance-based compensation and employee training.

CRM packages typically include tools for three core functions:

1 · Sales force automation (SFA)
Helps sales staff increase productivity by focusing efforts on the most profitable customers. Provides sales prospect & contact information, product information, product configuration and sales quote generation.
2 · Customer service
Increases the efficiency of call centers, help desks and support staff. Assigns and manages customer service requests; offers web-based self-service capabilities.
3 · Marketing
Supports direct-marketing campaigns: captures prospect/customer data, provides product info, qualifies leads, schedules and tracks mailings/e-mail across e-mail, direct mail, telephone, web and social media.
Marketing analytics inside CRM CRM marketing tools analyze marketing and customer data to identify profitable and unprofitable customers, design products and services for specific customer needs, and identify opportunities for cross-selling.

Operational vs Analytical CRM

The single most examinable distinction in this lecture. Operational CRM includes the customer-facing applications — the tools employees and customers directly use: sales force automation, call center and customer service support, and marketing automation. Analytical CRM includes applications that analyze the customer data generated by operational CRM to provide information for improving business performance — including the crucial Customer Lifetime Value (CLTV), which estimates the total worth of a customer relationship over its whole life. Operational CRM captures the interactions; analytical CRM learns from them.

Enterprise Applications: New Opportunities and Challenges

Enterprise applications are powerful but demanding. They are highly expensive to purchase and implement — typically $3.5 million to over $12 million over 16 months (for mid-size firms). Beyond cost, firms face technological changes, business process changes, and organizational changes; high switching costs and dependence on software vendors; and the unglamorous but essential work of data standardization, management and cleansing — dirty data in, dirty results out.

Next-generation enterprise applications

The industry is moving to make applications more flexible, Web-enabled, and integrated with other systems. Key directions include enterprise suites — software that enables CRM, SCM and enterprise systems to work together and with supplier/client systems using Web services and SOA; open-source and on-demand (SaaS) solutions; mobile-compatible software with Web 2.0 capabilities; complementary analytics products; and cloud-based software/services. A service platform integrates multiple applications to deliver a seamless experience for all parties — e.g. the order-to-cash process. Portal software integrates information from enterprise applications and legacy systems and presents it as if coming from a single source.

Social CRM

Social CRM enables a business to connect customer conversations and relationships from social networking sites to CRM processes. Its capabilities include linking data from social networks, social media analytics, campaign management and social marketing. Where traditional CRM records transactions the firm controls, social CRM listens and responds where the customers actually talk — turning public conversations into relationship intelligence.

Business Intelligence (for enterprise applications)

To help managers obtain more meaningful information from the massive amounts of data generated by enterprise systems, business intelligence tools provide flexible reporting, ad-hoc analysis, interactive dashboards, "what-if" scenario analysis and data visualization techniques. Three types of knowledge in the enterprise

Enterprise content comes in three forms: structured documents (reports, presentations, formal rules), semi-structured documents (e-mails, videos) and unstructured, tacit knowledge. The key statistic: about 80% of an organization's business content is semi-structured or unstructured — which is why enterprise-wide KM systems are so important and so hard.

Enterprise content management systems

Enterprise content management systems help capture, store, retrieve, distribute and preserve documents, reports, best practices and semi-structured knowledge (e-mails), bring in external sources (news feeds, research), and provide tools for communication and collaboration (blogs, wikis). The key problem is developing a taxonomy — knowledge objects must be tagged with categories so they can be found again. Digital asset management systems are specialized content management systems for classifying, storing and managing unstructured digital data such as photographs, graphics, video and audio.

Locating expertise and learning management

Enterprise-wide KM also includes locating and sharing expertise: online directories of corporate experts in well-defined knowledge domains, search tools that find the right expert, social networking tools for finding knowledge outside the firm, and tools for saving, tagging and sharing web pages. Learning management systems (LMS) provide tools for the management, delivery, tracking and assessment of employee learning and training. They support multiple modes of learning (CD-ROM, web-based classes, online forums, live instruction), automate course selection and administration, assemble and deliver learning content, measure learning effectiveness — and increasingly offer AI tutors, adaptive learning and personalized pathways. MOOCs (massively open online courses) are web courses open to very large numbers of participants.

Knowledge workers — researchers, designers, architects, scientists, engineers — create knowledge for the organization. They play three key roles: keeping the organization current in knowledge as it develops; serving as internal consultants regarding their areas of expertise; and acting as change agents — evaluating, initiating and promoting change projects. KWS are the systems built for them — to help create new knowledge and integrate that knowledge into the business.

Intelligent Techniques

Intelligent techniques are used to capture individual and collective knowledge and extend the knowledge base. The traditional approach assigns techniques by purpose: to capture tacit knowledge — expert systems, case-based reasoning, fuzzy logic; for knowledge discovery — neural networks and data mining; to generate solutions to complex problems — genetic algorithms; and to automate tasks — intelligent agents.

Expert systems

Expert systems capture tacit knowledge in a very specific and limited domain of human expertise, capturing the knowledge of skilled employees as a set of rules in a software system that others can use. They typically perform limited, discrete, highly structured tasks that take minutes or hours — e.g. diagnosing a malfunctioning machine or deciding whether to grant a credit loan. An expert system consists of a knowledge base (a set of hundreds or thousands of rules) and an inference engine — the strategy used to search the knowledge base. The engine works by "firing" rules triggered by facts entered by the user, like deeply nested IF statements at massive scale. Rules are interconnected, the number of outcomes is known in advance and limited, and multiple paths can reach the same outcome.

Forward chaining
Inference engine begins with the information entered by the user and searches the knowledge base to arrive at a conclusion
Backward chaining
Begins with a hypothesis and asks the user questions until the hypothesis is confirmed or disproved
Expert system limitations Most deal with classification problems with few known outcomes, and many require large, lengthy, expensive development and maintenance — sometimes hiring or training more experts is cheaper than building the system.

Case-based reasoning (CBR)

CBR stores descriptions of past experiences of human specialists — "cases" — in a knowledge base. When a new problem arises, the system searches for cases with similar characteristics and applies the old solutions to the new case; successful and unsuccessful applications are grouped with the case so the knowledge base is continuously expanded and refined by users — storing organizational intelligence. CBR uses a six-step process to generate solutions, and is found in medical diagnostic systems and customer support.

Fuzzy logic

Fuzzy logic is a rule-based technology that represents imprecision, using linguistic categories (e.g. "cold", "cool") that represent a range of values. You describe a phenomenon linguistically, then represent that description in a small number of flexible rules — solving problems where expertise is hard to express in crisp IF-THEN rules. Applications: autofocus in cameras and detecting possible medical fraud. Membership functions translate linguistic expressions like "warm" into numbers a computer can manipulate (e.g. in a thermostat's temperature control logic).

Machine learning & neural networks

Machine learning is how computer programs improve performance without explicit programming — by recognizing patterns, from experience, and from prior learnings in databases. Contemporary examples: Google searches and the recommender systems of Amazon and Netflix. Neural networks find patterns and relationships in massive amounts of data too complicated for humans to analyze. They "learn" by searching for relationships, building models, and correcting over and over; humans train the network by feeding it data inputs whose outputs are known so it learns by example (e.g. distinguishing valid and fraudulent credit card purchases). Uses include pattern classification, prediction, financial analysis, control and optimization. Deep learning networks are more complex — many layers of transformation of input data to produce an output — and are used almost exclusively for pattern detection on unlabeled data, where the system discovers patterns without being told what to look for.

Genetic algorithms

Genetic algorithms are useful for finding the optimal solution to a specific problem by examining a very large number of possible solutions. They are conceptually based on evolution: searching among solution variables by changing and reorganizing component parts using inheritance, mutation and selection — starting from an initial "population" of chromosomes (candidate solutions) and iteratively refining so higher-fitness solutions emerge. They excel at optimization problems — minimizing costs, efficient scheduling, optimal jet engine design — where hundreds or thousands of variables exist, evaluating many alternatives quickly.

NLP, computer vision, robotics & intelligent agents

Natural language processing (NLP) makes it possible for a computer to understand and analyze natural language. Computer vision systems deal with how computers can emulate the human visual system to view and extract information from real-world images. Robotics deals with the design, construction, operation and use of movable machines that substitute for humans, with computer systems for control, sensory feedback and information processing. Intelligent agents work without direct human intervention to carry out specific, repetitive, predictable tasks for a user, process or application — deleting junk e-mail, finding the cheapest airfare — using a limited built-in or learned knowledge base; some self-adjust (e.g. Siri). Agent-based modeling uses systems of autonomous agents to model the behavior of consumers, stock markets and supply chains, and to predict the spread of epidemics — famously, intelligent agents helped P&G shorten replenishment cycles for products like a box of Tide.

Hybrid AI and the modern approach

Hybrid AI systems integrate genetic algorithms, fuzzy logic, neural networks and expert systems into a single application to take advantage of the best features of each — e.g. Matsushita's "neurofuzzy" washing machine combining fuzzy logic with neural networks. The lecture's modern approach to intelligent techniques includes: machine learning and deep learning (transformers, reinforcement learning); generative AI (GPT, Llama, Gemini, Claude); multimodal AI (text, image, video, speech); Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) — augmenting generation with retrieved knowledge; and autonomous AI agents (AutoGPT, BabyAGI, CrewAI).

Matching table — technique to purpose (very high exam frequency) Tacit knowledge capture → expert systems, CBR, fuzzy logic · Knowledge discovery → neural networks, data mining · Complex optimization solutions → genetic algorithms · Task automation → intelligent agents.

Decision Making: Why Information Changes the Answer

The lecture opens with a brilliant example that shows how more information changes decisions. A supplier must choose between two wristwatches. Knowing only the sales price (Casio 10,000/= vs Citizen 18,000/=), the Citizen looks better. Adding the cost (6,000/= vs 10,000/=), both yield a 4,000/= and 8,000/= margin respectively — Citizen still wins. But adding the sales forecast (2,000 units vs 800 units) flips everything: total profit is Casio 8,000,000/= versus Citizen 6,400,000/= — the Casio is more profitable. The lesson: improving hundreds of thousands of "small" decisions with better information adds up to large annual value for the business.

✏️ Worked example — the wristwatch decision

Option 1: Casio — sales price 10,000/=, cost 6,000/=, forecast 2,000 units. Option 2: Citizen — sales price 18,000/=, cost 10,000/=, forecast 800 units. Which product is more profitable to the supplier, and why did the answer change?

Solution

Unit profit: Casio = 10,000 − 6,000 = 4,000/=; Citizen = 18,000 − 10,000 = 8,000/=. Total profit: Casio = 4,000 × 2,000 = 8,000,000/=; Citizen = 8,000 × 800 = 6,400,000/=. Despite the higher unit margin, the Casio is more profitable overall because of volume. Each new piece of information (price → cost → forecast) changed the rational choice — the core argument for investing in decision-supporting information systems.

Why IT Investments Don't Always Pay Off

Three main reasons why investments in information technology do not always produce positive results: (1) Information quality — high-quality decisions require high-quality information; garbage in, garbage out. (2) Management filters — managers have selective attention and a variety of biases that reject information not conforming to prior conceptions. (3) Organizational inertia and politics — strong forces within organizations resist making decisions that call for major change.

High-velocity automated decision making

Some structured decisions are now made entirely by computers. High-velocity automated decision making is made possible through computer algorithms that precisely define the steps for a highly structured decision — with humans taken out of the decision. Example: high-speed computer trading programs that execute trades in 30 milliseconds — and were responsible for the "Flash Crash" of 2010. Such systems require safeguards to ensure proper operation and regulation.

Securing Information Systems

Lecture 09 · Threats & vulnerabilities, controls, encryption, firewalls, laws & audits

Security and Controls: Definitions

Two precise definitions open the lecture and both are directly examinable. Security refers to the policies, procedures, and technical measures used to prevent unauthorized access, alteration, theft, or physical damage to information systems. Controls are the methods, policies, and organizational procedures that ensure the safety of the organization's assets, the accuracy and reliability of its accounting records, and operational adherence to management standards. In short: security prevents bad things from happening; controls are the broader methods that keep the whole organization safe and accurate.

Why Systems Are Vulnerable

Contemporary security challenges exist everywhere a web application touches: a web client, a server, corporate information systems linked to databases — each component has vulnerabilities, and floods, fires, power failures and other electrical problems can disrupt the network at any point. The lecture lists six general reasons systems are vulnerable: (1) accessibility of networks, (2) hardware problems (breakdowns, configuration errors, damage from improper use or crime), (3) software problems (programming errors, installation errors, unauthorized changes), (4) disasters, (5) use of networks/computers outside the firm's control, and (6) loss and theft of portable devices.

Internet & wireless vulnerabilities

The Internet is open to anyone, and its size means abuses can have wide impact. The use of fixed Internet addresses with cable/DSL modems creates fixed targets for hackers. Unencrypted VoIP can be intercepted; e-mail and P2P carry interception risks, attachments with malicious software, and the risk of transmitting trade secrets. Wireless adds more: radio frequency bands are easy to scan. SSIDs (service set identifiers) identify access points, are broadcast multiple times, and can be detected by sniffer programs. War driving means eavesdroppers drive by buildings trying to detect the SSID and gain access to the network and its resources. Once an access point is breached, an intruder can use the operating system to access networked drives and files.

Hackers and Computer Crime

Hackers vs crackers: hacking activities include system intrusion, system damage, and cyber vandalism — the intentional disruption, defacement, or destruction of a web site or corporate information system. Key attack techniques: Spoofing — misrepresenting oneself using fake e-mail addresses or masquerading as someone else, or redirecting a web link to an address different from the intended one with the site masquerading as the intended destination. A sniffer is an eavesdropping program that monitors information traveling over a network, enabling hackers to steal proprietary information such as e-mail and company files.

Denial-of-service attacks & botnets

A denial-of-service (DoS) attack floods a server with thousands of false requests to crash the network — attackers gain nothing except the satisfaction of halting service. A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack uses numerous computers to launch the DoS. Botnets are networks of "zombie" PCs infiltrated by bot malware; botnets deliver 90 percent of the world's spam and 80 percent of its malware. The Mirai botnet is the lecture's named example. DDoS traffic typically comes from these zombie machines, which makes the attacker hard to trace.

Computer crime: target vs instrument

Computer crime is "any violation of criminal law that involves a knowledge of computer technology for its perpetration, investigation, or prosecution." The computer may be the target of crime — breaching confidentiality of protected computerized data, accessing a system without authority, knowingly accessing a protected computer to commit fraud, intentionally causing damage, transmitting damaging code, or threatening damage. Or the computer may be the instrument of crime — theft of trade secrets, unauthorized copying of software or copyrighted IP (articles, books, music, video), schemes to defraud, e-mail threats or harassment, intercepting electronic communications, illegal access to stored e-mail/voice mail, or transmitting child pornography.

Internal threats & software vulnerability

Security threats often originate inside the organization: employees have inside knowledge, and sloppy security procedures and user lack of knowledge compound the risk. Social engineering means tricking employees into revealing their passwords by pretending to be legitimate members of the company who need information. On the software side, commercial software contains hidden bugs (program code defects) that create vulnerabilities — zero defects cannot be achieved because complete testing is impossible for large programs. Patches are small pieces of software that repair flaws, but exploits are often created faster than patches can be released and implemented. Even microprocessor designs can be faulty — poor security designs of chips let hackers access "protected" data, affecting nearly every computer chip manufactured in the last 20 years.

Business Value of Security and Control

Failed computer systems can lead to significant or total loss of business function. Firms are more vulnerable than ever because they hold confidential personal and financial data, trade secrets, new products and strategies. A security breach may cut into a firm's market value almost immediately, and inadequate security brings issues of liability. Legal and regulatory requirements for electronic records management and privacy include: HIPAA (medical security and privacy rules and procedures), the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (requires financial institutions to ensure the security and confidentiality of customer data), and the Sarbanes-Oxley Act (imposes responsibility on companies and their management to safeguard the accuracy and integrity of financial information used internally and released externally).

Electronic evidence: evidence for white-collar crimes is often in digital form — data on computers, e-mail, instant messages, e-commerce transactions — and proper control of data can save time and money when responding to legal discovery requests. Application controls are specific to each computerized application (such as payroll or order processing), include both automated and manual procedures, and ensure that only authorized data are completely and accurately processed by that application.

The Organizational Framework for Security and Control

Risk assessment determines the level of risk to the firm if a specific activity or process is not properly controlled — considering the types of threat, probability of occurrence during the year, potential losses (value of the threat), and the expected annual loss. A security policy ranks information risks, identifies acceptable security goals, and identifies mechanisms for achieving those goals. It drives other policies such as the acceptable use policy (AUP) — defining acceptable uses of the firm's information resources and computing equipment — and authorization policies — determining differing levels of user access to information assets.

Identity management refers to the business processes and tools for identifying valid users of the system and controlling their access — identifying and authorizing different categories of users, specifying which portions of the system each may access, and authenticating users while protecting their identities. Identity management systems capture access rules for different levels of users (e.g. security profiles in a personnel system). Disaster recovery planning devises plans for the restoration of disrupted services, while business continuity planning focuses on restoring business operations after a disaster. Both identify the firm's most critical systems, use business impact analysis to determine the impact of an outage, and management must determine which systems are restored first. An information systems audit examines the firm's overall security environment and the controls governing individual information systems — reviewing technologies, procedures, documentation, training and personnel; it may even simulate a disaster to test the response of technology and staff, and it lists, ranks and estimates the probability of control weaknesses and their financial and organizational impact.

Memory hook — recovery vs continuity Disaster recovery = restore the SYSTEMS/services; business continuity = restore the BUSINESS operations. Both plans are needed, both start from business impact analysis.

Tools and Technologies for Safeguarding Information Resources

1 · Authentication & identity management software

Identity management software automates keeping track of all users and privileges — authenticating users, protecting identities and controlling access. Authentication methods include password systems, tokens, smart cards, biometric authentication, and two-factor authentication (combining two independent methods, e.g. password + OTP token).

2 · Firewalls, intrusion detection & antivirus

A firewall is a combination of hardware and software that prevents unauthorized users from accessing private networks — placed between the firm's private network and the public Internet. Its technologies include static packet filtering, stateful inspection, network address translation (NAT), and application proxy filtering. Intrusion detection systems (IDS) monitor hot spots on corporate networks to detect and deter intruders, examining events as they happen to discover attacks in progress. Antivirus and antispyware software check computers for the presence of malware and can often eliminate it — but require continual updating. Unified threat management (UTM) systems combine multiple security functions in a single appliance.

3 · Securing wireless networks

WEP security can provide some protection by assigning a unique name to the network's SSID and not broadcasting the SSID, and by using WEP together with VPN technology. The Wi-Fi Alliance finalized the WPA2 specification, replacing WEP with stronger standards — continually changing keys and an encrypted authentication system with a central server.

4 · Encryption

Encryption is the process of transforming text or data into cipher text that cannot be read by unintended recipients. Two methods for encryption on networks are Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) and its successor Transport Layer Security (TLS), and Secure Hypertext Transfer Protocol (S-HTTP). There are also two ways to encrypt the data itself:

Symmetric key encryption
Sender and receiver use a single, shared key — fast, but the key must be exchanged securely
Public key encryption
Two mathematically related keys: the sender encrypts with the recipient's public key; the recipient decrypts with their private key

A digital certificate is a data file used to establish the identity of users and electronic assets for protection of online transactions. It uses a trusted third party — a certification authority (CA) — to validate a user's identity; the CA verifies identity, stores the information in its server, and generates an encrypted digital certificate containing the owner's ID information and a copy of the owner's public key. Public key infrastructure (PKI) is the use of public key cryptography working with a certificate authority — widely used in e-commerce.

5 · Availability, network traffic & outsourcing

Ensuring system availability matters because online transaction processing requires 100% availability — no downtime. Fault-tolerant computer systems provide continuous availability (e.g. stock markets) using redundant hardware, software and power supply components so service continues uninterrupted through failures. Deep packet inspection (DPI) controls network traffic — examining data files and sorting low-priority material, and able to block video and music downloads. Security outsourcing uses managed security service providers (MSSPs) to run security operations.

6 · Cloud, mobile & software quality

For cloud security, responsibility resides with the company owning the data. Firms must ensure providers give adequate protection: where data are stored, meeting corporate requirements and legal privacy laws, segregation of data from other clients, audits and security certifications — governed by service level agreements (SLAs). For mobile platforms, security policies must cover mobile devices with guidelines for platforms and applications; mobile device management (MDM) tools handle authorization, inventory records, control updates, lock down/erase lost devices and encryption; software can segregate corporate data on devices. Finally, ensuring software quality uses software metrics — objective, quantified measurements of the system (number of transactions, online response time, payroll checks printed per hour, known bugs per hundred lines of code) — plus early and regular testing, walkthroughs (review of specification or design documents by a small group of qualified people) and debugging (the process by which errors are eliminated).

✏️ Worked example — build a defense-in-depth plan

A bank's online system holds customer records and transaction data. For each threat, name the best tool from this lecture: (a) attackers flooding the web server with false requests from thousands of machines; (b) an employee tricked into revealing a password over the phone; (c) interception of customer logins on a public Wi-Fi hotspot; (d) the bank needing proof of transactions for court.

Solution
  • (a) Firewall (with stateful inspection / DPI) + DDoS-aware design — DoS/DDoS floods the server with false requests; botnets of zombie PCs launch it.
  • (b) Security awareness / anti-social-engineering training + AUP — social engineering tricks employees into revealing passwords by pretending to be legitimate members of the company.
  • (c) Encryption — SSL/TLS (or S-HTTP) and caution against "evil twin" hotspots that pretend to offer trustworthy Wi-Fi.
  • (d) Computer forensics + electronic evidence controls — scientific collection, examination, authentication, preservation and analysis of data for use as evidence in court.
🧠 Topic 9 Quiz — Securing Information Systems
12 questions · answer every question, then press Check answers

1. Which statement best defines information systems security?

Security = policies, procedures and technical measures to prevent unauthorized access, alteration, theft or physical damage. Controls = methods, policies and organizational procedures ensuring safety of assets, accuracy/reliability of accounting records and adherence to management standards.

2. An independent program that copies itself from computer to computer over a network is a:

Worms are independent and self-copying over networks. Viruses attach to other programs or data files; trojans appear benign but misbehave; sniffers eavesdrop on network traffic.

3. "War driving" refers to:

War driving: driving by buildings and trying to detect the SSID (broadcast multiple times, findable by sniffer programs) to access network resources. Once an access point is breached, intruders can reach networked drives and files.

4. A DDoS attack differs from a DoS attack because it:

DDoS = distributed — many machines (zombie PCs in botnets, which deliver ~90% of spam and ~80% of malware; e.g. Mirai) flood the target with false requests.

5. Setting up fake web sites or e-mails that look like legitimate businesses to obtain confidential personal data is:

Phishing = fake sites/e-mails that look legitimate to harvest confidential data. Pharming redirects users to a bogus page even when they type the correct address; spoofing = misrepresenting identity; click fraud = fraudulent ad clicks.

6. "Theft of trade secrets using e-mail" is an example of the computer as:

When the computer facilitates the crime (stealing trade secrets, e-mail threats, fraud schemes, illegal interception), it is the instrument. When the computer itself is attacked (unauthorized access, data breaches, damaging code), it is the target.

7. Which law requires financial institutions to ensure the security and confidentiality of customer data?

Gramm-Leach-Bliley = financial institutions' customer data. HIPAA = medical security/privacy. Sarbanes-Oxley = accuracy and integrity of financial information.

8. Input, processing and output controls are examples of:

Application controls are unique to each computerized application (payroll, order processing) and include input, processing and output controls. General controls (software, hardware, computer operations, data security, implementation, administrative) apply to all applications.

9. In public key encryption, the sender encrypts the message using:

Public key encryption uses two mathematically related keys: sender encrypts with the recipient's PUBLIC key (found in a directory); recipient decrypts with their PRIVATE key. A single shared key = symmetric encryption.

10. A certification authority (CA) in a digital certificate system:

The CA verifies the user's identity, stores the information in its server, and generates an encrypted digital certificate containing the owner's ID information and a copy of the owner's public key. PKI = public key cryptography + CA, widely used in e-commerce.

11. A stock market system with redundant hardware, software and power supplies that provides continuous, uninterrupted service is:

Fault-tolerant systems contain redundant hardware, software and power supply components creating an environment of continuous, uninterrupted service — required where online transaction processing demands 100% availability.

12. "Known bugs per hundred lines of code" and "online response time" are examples of:

Software metrics are objective assessments of systems in the form of quantified measurements — transactions, response time, payroll checks printed per hour, known bugs per hundred lines of code. They support early/regular testing, walkthroughs and debugging.

1. All of the following are technology components of an information system EXCEPT:

Technology components: hardware, software, data management technology, networking/telecom. Organizational culture belongs to the organization dimension.

From Lecture 03: organizational/political resistance to change is the most common reason for failure of large projects.

Executive Support Systems (ESS) — e.g. digital dashboards showing working capital, receivables, payables, cash flow, inventory.

4. In Porter's Five Forces, "exit barriers" such as redundancy payments and government pressure belong to:

Exit barriers keep firms competing in an industry — a determinant of rivalry among existing competitors.

5. Continuous improvement and innovation in the value chain belong to which support activity?

Technological development covers product & process design, continuous improvement and innovation.

6. Cookies and web site privacy policies are directly concerned with which moral dimension?

Privacy = information rights and obligations: what information rights individuals possess and what they can protect.

7. A just-in-time strategy in supply chain management means:

JIT minimizes inventory — components arrive exactly when needed, finished goods ship immediately. Safety stock is the opposite (buffer against inflexibility).

8. Enterprise software embodies:

Enterprise software is built around predefined best-practice processes; firms select functions and map their processes to them using configuration tables.

9. Customer Lifetime Value (CLTV) is computed by:

Analytical CRM analyzes data generated by operational CRM — providing CLTV and other performance insights.

10. Which of the following is a specialized system for managing unstructured digital data like photographs, video and audio?

Digital asset management systems classify, store and manage unstructured digital media — a specialized form of content management.

11. CAD systems and virtual reality systems are examples of:

KWS are specialized systems for knowledge workers (engineers, scientists, designers) who create new knowledge — CAD, VR, investment workstations.

12. Genetic algorithms are BEST suited for:

Genetic algorithms use evolution-inspired processes (inheritance, mutation, selection) to find optimal solutions among huge solution spaces — e.g. cost minimization, scheduling, jet engine design.

13. In the decision-making process, "choosing among solution alternatives" is the ______ stage.

Intelligence = understand the problem; Design = identify/explore solutions; Choice = pick one; Implementation = make it work and monitor.

14. A manager allocating budgets among departments is performing which Mintzberg role?

Resource allocator — a decisional role — decides who gets money, staff and time.

15. Business intelligence infrastructure includes databases, data warehouses and:

BI infrastructure = databases, data warehouses, data marts. Analytics tools (OLAP, statistics, models, data mining) sit on top of it.

16. A fake wireless network that pretends to offer trustworthy Wi-Fi is called a(n):

Evil twins impersonate legitimate Wi-Fi hotspots to capture victims' traffic and credentials.

17. Sarbanes-Oxley primarily concerns:

Sarbanes-Oxley imposes responsibility on companies and management to safeguard the accuracy and integrity of financial information used internally and released externally. Medical = HIPAA; financial institution customer data = Gramm-Leach-Bliley.

18. Which pair correctly matches control type to scope?

General controls govern the whole IT infrastructure (software, hardware, operations, data security, implementation, administrative). Application controls (input/processing/output) are unique to each application.

19. SSL/TLS and S-HTTP are methods for:

The two methods for encryption on networks: Secure Sockets Layer (SSL)/Transport Layer Security (TLS) and Secure Hypertext Transfer Protocol (S-HTTP).

20. In the knowledge management value chain, portals, wikis, search engines and collaboration tools serve which stage?

Dissemination spreads knowledge through portals, wikis, e-mail/IM, search engines, collaboration tools, training programs and informal networks.
