Introduction to Business Economics

Lesson 01 · Tutorial 01 · V.P.V. Audarika

What is Economics?

The word Economics originates from the Greek word “Oikonomikos”, which can be divided into two parts: “Oikos” meaning House and “Nomos” meaning Management. So economics literally means “household management” — deciding how to run an estate with limited means. Economics is often called the queen of social sciences because its concepts spill over into every other study of human behaviour, from politics to geography to law.

Adam Smith (Father of Economics): Economics is the “science of the wealth” — the study of the nature and uses of national wealth.
Dr. Alfred Marshall: “Economics is a study of man’s actions in the ordinary business of life; it enquires how he gets his income and how he uses it.”
Prof. Lionel Robbins: “The science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses.”
Modern working definition: Economics is the study of how people allocate their limited resources to produce goods and services to satisfy their endless wants and to maximise their gains.
Business Economics: the application of economic theory and methodology to solving business problems — pricing, production, forecasting and decision-making inside firms.

The Foundation Concept: Scarcity

Scarcity is the foundation concept of economics. Resources are very limited compared to unlimited human needs and wants. For an individual, resources include time, money and skill; for a business, resources enable production of goods and services; for a country, limited resources include natural resources, capital, labour force and technology.

Four points to remember about scarcity
  • Scarcity is a common concept for every society — rich and poor countries alike face it.
  • Scarcity is a relative concept — resources are scarce relative to wants, not in absolute terms.
  • Scarcity does not reflect inefficiencies — even an efficient economy cannot escape it.
  • Scarcity does not mean poverty — a wealthy person or nation still faces scarcity of time and resources.

Needs vs Wants

Needs are essential for survival (food, water, shelter, basic clothing); wants are desires that are unlimited and ever-growing — you may need transport, but you want a luxury car. Because wants are unlimited while resources are limited, every society must choose. This is where the whole economic problem begins.

🎯 Choice

Choice is the process of deciding which needs of people in society will be met using the scarce resources available. The question of choice arises for two reasons: (1) lack of resources, and (2) the existence of alternative uses for resources.

⚖ Trade-off

You can’t have it all (scarcity — remember?), so you must choose how to spend your money, time and energy. Every decision means picking one thing over all other possibilities — a trade-off.

Opportunity Cost

Opportunity cost refers to the value of the next best option that must be sacrificed when making a choice among alternatives in an economic activity. If you spend your evening studying economics, the opportunity cost is the leisure (or wages) you gave up — not all the other things combined, only the best one sacrificed.

Things to consider when evaluating opportunity cost
  • Opportunity cost is a concrete concept — it can be identified precisely.
  • Opportunity cost is a subjective concept — it differs from person to person.
  • It is not limited to the person making the decision — it can affect others too.
When is opportunity cost zero? (1) for free goods; (2) when there are no alternatives; (3) in a situation of general unemployment (idle resources can be used without sacrificing anything).

Philosophy of Economics: Positive vs Normative

🔬 Positive Economics

  • Answers what is? what happened? what will happen?
  • A statement of fact showing the relationship between variables
  • Accuracy can be tested by empirical evidence; if it does not match evidence, the statement is rejected
  • Ex: “There is an inverse relationship between money supply and price level”; “Sri Lankan inflation rate was 6.8% last year”

💫 Normative Economics

  • Focuses on what ought to be — value judgements
  • Statements are subjective, cannot be tested against evidence
  • Often contain the words should / must / ought
  • Ex: “Income should be distributed equally”; “Minimum wages should be increased”

Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

Microeconomics

Focuses on analysing the behaviour of individual units in an economic system — consumers, workers, production institutions and government.

  • A customer deciding to buy a smartphone based on price, features and budget
  • A farmer deciding how much wheat to produce based on market prices

Macroeconomics

The analysis of the structure, performance and behaviour of an economic system as a whole.

  • Inflation · Unemployment · Balance of payments · Exchange rate · National output

The Three Fundamental Economic Questions

Every society — developed or developing — faces three basic economic problems that arise from the central problem of scarcity:

1. What to produce?

An economy wants many things, but all cannot be produced with available resources. The economy must choose which goods to produce and which not to.

2. How to produce?

Which technique should be used? Various techniques are available — e.g. for wheat we may use more labour and less capital, or less labour and more capital.

3. For whom to produce?

The problem of distribution: who consumes the output depends on how the National Product is distributed among households.

Why do they arise?

All three trace back to scarcity + choice: unlimited wants, limited resources, and alternative uses of those resources.

Scarcity forces choice, choice creates trade-offs, and trade-offs create opportunity cost — the chain that drives all of economics.

If you choose the cinema, the opportunity cost is the value of the tutoring class — the next best alternative sacrificed — not the money you spent on the ticket alone. Opportunity cost includes both explicit and implicit (time) sacrifices.

Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

Lesson 02 · Tutorial 02 · Chathuranga Adhikari

From Scarcity to the PPC

The primary study of economics is how the scarce resources of an economy should be used to satisfy unlimited human wants. To gain the maximum outcome from available resources, society must achieve both full employment and full production:

Full employment

Utilising all resources that can be used for current production (this does not affect sustainability, since it uses current resources).

Full production

Every production factor used in the production process must contribute to its maximum to meet the physical needs of customers.

Even an economy geared to both goals cannot produce unlimited output — it must decide which goods to produce and which to sacrifice. This is fundamentally a question of choice, and the simplest model used to analyse choice and its consequences is the production possibilities model, presented as a table or a graph.

Definition A Production Possibility Curve (PPC) — also called Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) — is a graphical representation of all possible maximum combinations of total output that could be produced when all resources are fully and efficiently used with a given technology.

The Four Assumptions of the PPC

  1. Resources are fixed over the period under consideration.
  2. Resources are used to achieve full employment and productive efficiency.
  3. Technology remains unchanged over the period.
  4. The economy produces only two products.

Reading Points on the PPC

✅ Efficient production

All resources are fully employed; producing the most possible. On the frontier it is impossible to produce more of one item without producing less of the other.

⚠ Inefficient production

Points inside the curve — not all resources are employed (underemployment/unemployment). Both goods can still be increased. Occurs during recession, depression, or in developing countries.

🚫 Unattainable points

Any point outside the curve is impossible — more of both goods cannot be produced with current resources.

⚖ Trade-offs

Any two or more points on the frontier represent trade-offs — the PPC shows choice and the opportunity cost of choosing one alternative over another.

Two types of efficiencyProductive efficiency — producing the maximum output from given resources (any point ON the PPC); allocative efficiency — producing the combination that society values most (the single best point on the PPC, which depends on preferences).

Using the table above, find: (1) the opportunity cost of moving A→B; (2) B→D; (3) D→B; (4) what can you say about a point G beyond the curve?

  1. A→B: computers rise 0→2, bikes fall 14→12. Opportunity cost = 2 bikes (for 2 extra computers).

  4. Point G lies outside the frontier — it is unattainable with current resources and technology.
Why is the PPC bowed outward (concave)? Because of the law of increasing opportunity cost: resources are not equally suited to both goods. As more computers are produced, resources increasingly less suited to computers (and better suited to bikes) must be diverted, so each extra computer costs progressively more bikes.

Shifting the PPC — 4 Assumptions Revisited

The PPC is drawn assuming fixed resources, full employment, and fixed technology. If any of these changes, the curve shifts. There are three shifters:

1. Change in resource quantity or quality

More/better resources → outward shift; fewer resources (disaster, emigration) → inward shift. Education improves labour quality → outward.

2. Change in technology

Technology improving in one industry pivots the curve out for that good only; broad improvements shift the whole curve out.

3. Change in trade

Specialisation and trade let an economy consume beyond its own PPC even though it cannot produce beyond it.

⚠ What does NOT shift the PPC?

A change in demand for the goods, and unemployment — these move the economy to a different point (inside) the same curve.

Economic growth shifts the PPC outward; loss of resources (disaster, emigration of professionals) shifts it inward.
✏️ Worked example PPC practice — draw & explain

Draw a PPC showing the change for each scenario (Pizza & Robots; then Consumer goods & Capital goods) and explain why:

  1. New robot-making technology
  2. Decrease in the demand for pizza
  3. Mad cow disease kills 85% of cows
  4. Destruction of power plants → severe electricity shortage
  5. Faster computer hardware
  6. Many workers are unemployed
  7. Significant increases in education
  1. New robot-making technology — the curve shifts outward only on the robot axis (pivot): more robots can be produced at every pizza level.
  2. Decrease in demand for pizza — the curve does not shift! A change in demand moves the economy along the same PPC; production possibilities are unchanged.
  3. Mad cow disease kills 85% of cows — a resource (cattle) is destroyed, so the curve shifts inward on the pizza axis only (pivot toward origin).
  4. Destruction of power plants — fewer capital resources → production possibilities fall for both goods: inward shift of the whole curve.
  5. Faster computer hardware — quality of a resource improves → the curve shifts outward for the good using that resource.
  6. Many workers unemployed — the curve does not shift! Unemployment is a point inside the curve, not a change in capacity.
  7. Significant increases in education — quality of labour improves → the curve shifts outward for the whole economy.

Importance and Limitations of the PPC

Importance

  • Since productive resources are always limited, the PPC helps decide which commodities are most beneficial to society.
  • Helps decide what amount of goods to produce among different alternatives.

Limitations

  • There is a choice between only two commodities.
  • It talks about production capabilities but doesn’t consider the want for goods.
  • It doesn’t consider the satisfaction generated with an accepted outcome.

Q2. List four assumptions of the production possibility curve.

Solution
  1. Resources are fixed over the period.
  2. Full employment and productive efficiency of resources.
  3. Technology is unchanged.
  4. Only two goods are produced.

Q3. What are the reasons for the increasing opportunity cost in the use of production resources?

Solution
Resources are not equally efficient in all uses (they are specialised or heterogeneous). As production of one good expands, the economy must divert resources increasingly less suited to it — each extra unit therefore requires sacrificing more and more of the other good. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost, which gives the PPC its bowed-out (concave) shape.

Q4. Draw and explain the following using PPFs (economy produces rice and clothes):

Solution
  1. Return of professionals who had left — labour force grows → whole PPC shifts outward.
  2. Professionals leaving due to economic downturn — labour shrinks → whole PPC shifts inward.
  3. Destruction of capital resources by natural disaster — capital stock falls → PPC shifts inward.
  4. Road network expansion utilises unused rural resources — previously idle resources become employable → effective resource base grows → PPC shifts outward.
  5. Quality of education increases — labour quality improves → PPC shifts outward.
  6. Depletion of non-renewable resources — fewer natural resources → PPC shifts inward.
  7. Real GDP shrinks 8%, unemployment rises — productive capacity unchanged but operating point moves inside the PPC (the curve itself need not shift; the economy becomes inefficient).
  8. Prolonged power outages from drought — energy input effectively reduced → PPC shifts inward (capacity to produce falls).
  9. Rice industry expanding more than apparel — pivot: the rice intercept extends outward while the clothes intercept stays roughly fixed (biased growth toward rice).
  10. New fertiliser for paddy only — technology improves in rice alone → PPC pivots outward on the rice axis.

Demand, Supply & Market Equilibrium

Lesson 03 · Tutorial 03 · Chathuranga Adhikari

The Basic Decision-Making Units

Firm: an organization that transforms resources (inputs) into products (outputs); firms are the primary producing units in a market economy.
Entrepreneur: a person who organizes, manages and assumes the risks of a firm — taking a new idea or product and turning it into a successful business.
Household: the consuming units in an economy.

The Circular Flow of Economic Activity

The circular flow shows the connections between firms and households in input markets and output markets. Payments flow in the opposite direction to the physical flow of resources, goods and services.

  • Output (product) markets: markets in which goods and services are exchanged.
  • Input (factor) markets: markets in which resources — labour, capital and land — are exchanged.
The circular flow: physical flows (blue) run one way; money payments (amber) run the opposite way.
Input markets includeLabour market — households supply work for wages; capital market — households supply savings for interest or claims to future profits; land market — households supply land or real property in exchange for rent.

Demand

Demand is the various amounts of a product (goods or services) that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices during a specific period. A household’s demand decision depends on: the price of the product, household income, accumulated wealth, prices of related products, tastes and preferences, and expectations about future income, wealth and prices.

The Law of DemandThere is a negative (inverse) relationship between price and quantity demanded — demand curves slope downward. When price rises, quantity demanded falls; when price falls, quantity demanded rises (ceteris paribus).

A demand schedule is a table showing how much of a product a household would be willing to buy at different prices; the demand curve is its graph.

(a) Pizza slices: at $0.50 quantity = 8; price rises $0.25 per step and quantity falls 1 each step (to 2 at $2.00). Derive the demand equation.
(b) At P = 0, Q = 100; at P = 5, Q = 0. Derive the equation.
(c) A demand curve has intercepts P = 280 (Q=0) and Q = 28 (P=0). Derive equation and schedule.

(a) Slope = ΔQ/ΔP = (2−8)/(2.00−0.50) = −6/1.5 = −4. Using point ($0.50, 8): Q = a − 4P → 8 = a − 2 → a = 10.  Qd = 10 − 4P  (check P=2: Q=2 ✓)

(c) Slope = ΔQ/ΔP = (0−28)/(280−0) = −0.1.  Qd = 28 − 0.1P. Schedule: P=0→28, P=70→21, P=140→14, P=210→7, P=280→0.

The Demand Function (full determinants)

Qdx = f (Px, Y, P1…Pn−1, T, Pe, S)Px = own price · Y = income · P1…Pn−1 = prices of related goods · T = tastes & preferences · Pe = expectations · S = social factors

Income vs Wealth

Income — the sum of wages, salaries, profits, interest, rents and other earnings in a given period; a flow measure. Wealth (net worth) — total value of what a household owns minus what it owes; a stock measure.

Normal vs Inferior goods

Normal goods: demand rises when income rises, falls when income falls. Inferior goods: demand falls when income rises (e.g. used cars, public transport for some consumers).

Substitutes

Goods that serve as replacements for one another; when the price of one rises, demand for the other rises. Perfect substitutes are identical products. Ex: Dominos vs Pizza Hut, Nike vs Reebok, tea vs coffee, IBM vs Apple computers.

Complements

Goods that go together; a decrease in the price of one increases demand for the other. Ex: bread & butter, PlayStation & games, vehicles & fuel, cereal & milk, dress shirts & ties.

Shift vs Movement — the most tested idea in this topic
  • Change in the own price of the good → change in quantity demanded = movement along the same demand curve.
  • Change in income, preferences, prices of related goods, expectations, social factors → change in demand = shift of the whole curve (right = increase, left = decrease).
  • Higher income increases demand for normal goods but decreases demand for inferior goods.
  • Price of hamburgers rises → demand for substitute (chicken) shifts right; demand for complement (ketchup) shifts left.

Market demand is the sum of all quantities demanded per period by all households in the market — the horizontal summation of individual demand curves (add quantities at each price).

Supply

Supply is the amount of goods or services that sellers are willing and able to sell at various prices. A supply schedule shows how much firms supply at different prices; quantity supplied is the number of units offered for sale at a particular price during a given time.

The Law of SupplyThere is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied — supply curves slope upward.
Qs = a + bP  (in tutorial problems often Qs = −c + dP, c > 0)Supply function: Qsx = f (Px, Pf, P1…Pn−1, T, E, G) — own price, factor prices, related-goods prices, technology, supplier expectations, government policies

Market supply is the horizontal summation of individual firms’ supply curves — the sum of all quantities supplied per period by all firms selling in the market.

Shift vs Movement for supplyChange in the good’s own price → movement along the supply curve (change in quantity supplied). Change in costs, input prices, technology, prices of related goods, expectations, government policy → shift of the supply curve (change in supply).

Market Equilibrium

An equilibrium is a condition that exists when quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. At equilibrium there is no tendency for price to change. Only at P₀ do the wishes of buyers and sellers coincide.

⬆️ Excess demand (shortage)

Exists when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price (price below equilibrium). Price tends to rise until equilibrium is restored.

⬇️ Excess supply (surplus)

Exists when quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded (price above equilibrium). Price tends to fall until equilibrium is restored.

Government Intervention in Markets

Lesson 04 · Tutorial 04 (Parts I & II) · Chathuranga Adhikari

Why Intervene? Market Failures

Unregulated competitive markets usually lead to economic efficiency — maximisation of aggregate consumer and producer surplus. However, sometimes market failures occur, where prices fail to provide proper signals and an unregulated market becomes inefficient:

Externalities

Costs or benefits that do not show up in the market price (e.g. pollution). They are external to the market, so buyers and sellers ignore them.

Public goods

Goods that markets under-provide because no one can be excluded from benefiting — e.g. national defence, street lighting.

Monopolies

A single seller restricts output and raises price above the competitive level, creating deadweight loss.

Imperfect information

Prevents consumers from making utility-maximising decisions; producers may know more than buyers.

Seven methods of government intervention: (1) buffer stocks, (2) price controls (maximum & minimum prices), (3) taxation, (4) subsidies, (5) state/direct provision, (6) regulation.

1. Buffer Stocks

A buffer stock is a system that buys and stores stocks in good harvests (preventing prices from falling below a target level) and releases stocks in bad harvests (preventing prices from rising above a target level). It influences market supply by holding or releasing stocks to stabilise prices or incomes.

Buffer stock in action: after a bad harvest the government releases 50 units onto the market; after a good harvest it buys up 60 units — either way the price stays near TP.

Key features

  • A short-term measure — used in agriculture where supply is volatile
  • Assumes supply is perfectly inelastic in the short run
  • Only useful where goods can be stored
  • Income stabilisation schemes aim to keep farm incomes constant by manipulating price through releasing or adding to stores

Problems of buffer stock schemes

  • Farmers stop responding to market signals — the market becomes distorted
  • Overproduction if incomes are guaranteed
  • Issues in storing food; cost of storage
  • Farmers’ moral hazard — guaranteed constant income
  • Long-term sustainability and international effects (LDCs, WTO)

2. Price Ceilings (Maximum Price)

A price ceiling occurs when the government sets a legal maximum limit on the price of a good or service — the highest price a producer, group of producers or an industry may charge. The aim is to keep prices below the market equilibrium to protect consumers. To be effective, the ceiling must be set below the free-market price.

A maximum price Pₙₐₓ below equilibrium: supply contracts to Qₛ, demand expands to Qₜ → shortage → rationing, queues and a possible black market at a price far above equilibrium.

Advantages

  • More affordable price for consumers
  • Price stability
  • Greater demand / access

Disadvantages

  • Shortages (excess demand)
  • Lack of incentives for producers
  • Black market emerges
  • More unemployment in the industry
  • Less choice & quality decline

3. Price Floors (Minimum Price)

A price floor is a government-imposed legal minimum price — the lowest price at which a good or service may legally be sold. It is usually set above the equilibrium price to protect producers from prices deemed too low, ensuring they cover production costs and earn a reasonable income.

A minimum price Pₙₐₘ above equilibrium: demand falls to Qₜ, supply rises to Qₛ → surplus. Government may buy the surplus (e.g. buffer stock) or restrict production.

Advantages

  • Protects producers’ incomes
  • Encourages production
  • Corrects market failure
  • Reduces consumption of harmful goods

Disadvantages

  • Inefficient resource allocation
  • Consumers pay higher prices
  • Surplus production (waste or storage cost)

Consumer Surplus, Producer Surplus & Deadweight Loss

Consumer surplus (CS): the total benefit or value consumers receive beyond what they pay — measured by the area between the demand curve and the market price. If you were willing to pay $9 and the price is $5, you gain $4 of consumer surplus.
Producer surplus (PS): the total benefit or revenue producers receive beyond production cost — measured by the area between the supply curve and the market price. A producer willing to accept $3 who receives $5 gains $2.
Deadweight loss (DWL): the total loss in surplus (consumer + producer) when a policy pushes price above or below the market-clearing price — triangles B + C in the diagrams below. It measures the inefficiency of the policy.
At competitive equilibrium: CS (above price, below demand) + PS (below price, above supply) = total surplus, which is maximised.
CS = ½ × Q₀ × (Pintercept − P₀)   ·   PS = ½ × Q₀ × (P₀ − Ps-intercept)Pintercept = price where Qd = 0 (choke price); Ps-intercept = price where Qs = 0
Maximum price below equilibrium: quantity traded falls to Q₁; triangles B + C are the deadweight loss to society. Consumers who still buy gain area A (lower price); consumers who cannot buy lose B; producers lose A + C.

4. Taxation (Indirect / Goods Tax)

Government uses taxes and subsidies as financial tools to influence production and prices. A tax is a charge imposed on incomes, profits, or goods and services to fund government expenditure (e.g. stamp duty, income tax, GST, service tax). A per-unit tax on producers shifts the supply curve upward (left) by the amount of the tax.

A per-unit tax shifts supply up to Sₜₐₓ. Consumers pay Pₜ (more), producers keep Pₛ (less). The rectangle between them × Qₜ is government revenue; the triangle between Q₀ and Qₜ is the deadweight loss.
Tax — step recipe
  1. Find the original equilibrium (Qd = Qs) → P₀, Q₀.
  2. Write the new supply: producers now receive (P − tax), so Q₁ₛ = Q₁(P − t) — supply shifts left/up.
  3. Solve Qd = Q₁ₛ → new consumer price Pₜ and quantity Qₜ; producer price = Pₜ − t.
  4. Tax revenue = t × Qₜ.  DWL = ½ × (Q₀ − Qₜ) × t.

5. Subsidies

A subsidy is a direct payment or grant by the government to producers, making the price paid by consumers lower than it would otherwise be. Subsidised goods often benefit the community and might not be provided under the free market (e.g. certain foods, public transport, free school meals). A per-unit subsidy shifts supply downward (right).

A subsidy shifts supply right to Sₛₚₛ. Consumers pay a lower price Pₜ, producers receive a higher price Pₛ; the government pays the rectangle (subsidy × Qₜ) — larger than the combined gain in surplus, leaving a deadweight loss triangle.
Subsidy — step recipe
  1. Find original equilibrium P₀, Q₀.
  2. New supply: producers effectively receive (P + s): Q₁ₛ = Q₁(P + s) — supply shifts right/down.
  3. Solve for new consumer price Pₜ and quantity Qₜ; producer price = Pₜ + s.
  4. Government cost = s × Qₜ.  DWL = ½ × (Qₜ − Q₀) × s.

6. Direct Provision & 7. Regulation

Government / direct provision

Government takes over production of goods or services, in part or whole, supplying them directly — often free of charge. State-owned industries: electricity, water, railways. Also applies to mixed public-private sectors: education, hospitals.

Regulation

Various means by which government controls production and consumption: rules and laws applied to firms — controlling price, quality and quantity of goods produced or consumed. Ex: a doctor’s prescription required to buy controlled drugs.

Theory of Production & Cost

Lesson 05 · Tutorial 05 · Chathuranga Adhikari

The Firm and Production

A business firm is an organization, owned and operated by private individuals, that specialises in production. Production is the process of combining inputs to make outputs — the firm buys inputs from households or other firms and sells its output to consumers.

Profit of the firm = Sales revenue − Input costsThe firm must also deal with government: pays taxes, obeys laws & regulations, and receives public capital, legal and financial systems in return.

Inputs include labour, capital, land, raw materials, and other goods and services provided by other firms. Production does not necessarily involve physical conversion — lawyers, doctors and social workers convert intangible inputs into intangible outputs. The way inputs may be combined to produce output is the firm’s technology, and the production function tells us the maximum quantity of output obtainable from each combination of inputs over a period.

Short Run vs Long Run

Short run

A period in which the supply of certain inputs (plant, buildings, machines) is fixed or inelastic. Output can be increased only by increasing the variable input (typically labour). Fixed inputs remain constant regardless of output; variable inputs change with output. Total product = maximum output from a given combination of inputs.

Long run

A period in which the supply of all inputs is elastic — even “fixed” factors can change (but technology is given). Production can be increased by employing more of both variable and fixed inputs. In the long run there are no fixed inputs and no fixed costs.

Exam tipShort run vs long run is defined by input flexibility, not calendar time. To guide the firm over the next several years use the long-run lens; to decide what to do next week, use the short-run lens.

Total, Marginal and Average Product

MPL = ΔQ / ΔL   ·   APL = Q / LMarginal product of labour = rise in output when one more worker is hired (other inputs fixed). Average product = output per worker.

Costs: Key Distinctions

Sunk cost

A cost that already has been paid, or must be paid, regardless of any future action. Should never influence rational decisions. Even a future payment can be sunk if an unavoidable commitment to pay has been made.

Explicit vs implicit costs

Explicit: money actually paid out for inputs. Implicit: costs of inputs with no direct money payment — e.g. the owner’s own time and invested capital.

Economic vs accounting profit

Accounting profit = Total revenue − explicit costs. Economic profit = Total revenue − ALL economic costs (explicit + implicit). The difference between the two is the opportunity cost of owner-supplied resources — called normal profit, which is a cost of production.

Why it matters

If a firm earns economic losses (negative economic profit), owners could earn more elsewhere — in the long run firms leave the industry. If economic profit is exactly zero, the firm is earning normal profit and stays.

Short-Run Costs

Fixed costs (TFC)

Costs that do not vary with output — incurred even at zero production: rent, interest, depreciation, insurance, salaries. Also called supplementary, capacity, period or overhead costs.

Variable costs (TVC)

Costs that change with the quantity produced: materials, wages, power, stores. Also called prime or direct costs.

Why U-shaped curves? The production link
  • When MP rises, MC falls (output grows faster than cost); when MP falls, MC rises. Since MP ordinarily rises then falls, MC falls then rises — it is U-shaped.
  • At low output MC lies below AVC and ATC, pulling them down; at high output MC rises above them, pulling them up — hence AVC and ATC are U-shaped too.
  • MC intersects the minimum points of both AVC and ATC.
  • AFC always declines as Q grows (a fixed cost spread over more units) but never reaches zero.

The Long Run: Plant Size and Economies of Scale

In the long run the firm can change the size of its plant (its collection of fixed inputs). The LRATC (long-run average total cost) is the cost per unit when all inputs are variable — it combines the lowest portions of every short-run ATC curve available. For any output the firm chooses the plant size that minimises average cost; the long-run average cost can never be higher than short-run ATC.

The LRATC envelope: small plants (ATC₁), medium (ATC₂), large (ATC₃). Costs fall with scale, flatten, then rise.

Reasons for economies of scale

  • Technical economies — larger machines, specialisation
  • Managerial economies — specialised management
  • Risk-bearing economies — diversified output/markets
  • Marketing economies — bulk buying & advertising spread
  • Financial economies — cheaper finance for big firms

Reasons for diseconomies of scale

  • Managerial diseconomies — bureaucracy grows
  • Coordination and control problems
  • Workers’ alienation — loss of motivation in huge firms
  • Communication challenges — messages distort over layers
Scale vs returns — vocabulary checkEconomies of scale: LRATC falls as output increases. Constant returns: LRATC flat. Diseconomies: LRATC rises. These are long-run ideas — do not confuse with short-run increasing/diminishing marginal returns to a variable input.

Consumer Behaviour & Utility Maximization

Lesson 06 · Chathuranga Adhikari

Introduction: Rational Consumers

The theory of consumer behaviour explains how rational consumers decide the optimal combination of goods to consume with their limited budget in order to maximise their total utility at any given time. The purpose of utility analysis is to analyse how people behave rather than how they think — each consumer spends income in the way that yields the greatest satisfaction.

Utility

DefinitionUtility = satisfaction / happiness / pleasure one gets from consuming a good. Measured in personal units called “utils”.
  • Utility ≠ usefulness — a painting may be useless but give huge utility to an art lover.
  • Utility is subjective — it differs between people and situations.
  • Utility is difficult to quantify — hence the imaginary unit “util”.

Cardinal approach

Utility is measurable: numbers (utils) can be assigned to alternatives and added. E.g. “this cup of tea gives 10 utils”.

Ordinal approach

Utility is not measurable but comparable: alternatives can be ranked — first, second, third…. (Basis of indifference-curve analysis.)

Total Utility and Marginal Utility

Total utility (TU): the total benefit to a consumer from all units of a good purchased.
Marginal utility (MU): the extra benefit from the last unit purchased — the change in total utility from consuming one more unit. As the number of goods purchased rises, TU rises but MU falls.

The water-thirst story: each successive glass gives less extra satisfaction; when MU hits zero, TU is at its maximum; beyond that, extra units cause disutility.
Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility Added satisfaction declines as a consumer acquires additional units of a product. A first car transforms your life; a second is handy; a third barely matters — additional units are worth less and less in money terms, which is exactly why consumers will only buy more units if the price falls. Classic illustration: a thirsty person values the first glass of water enormously; each later glass less; forced further consumption creates disutility. Even for a free good, a rational consumer stops at the point where MU becomes negative (newspaper vending machines allow taking several papers because the MU of a second paper is effectively zero).

Consumer Choice and the Budget Constraint

Consumers’ incomes are limited because their resources are limited — this is the budget constraint. Goods have prices and are scarce relative to demand, so consumers must choose among alternatives with limited money income. The theory assumes: rational behaviour (maximise total utility), clear-cut preferences, a budget constraint, and every good has a price tag — so consumers must compromise.

Consumer Optimum for One Product

At consumer’s equilibrium (one good):   MU = P   and   M = P × QCondition 1: marginal utility equals price. Condition 2: total spending equals money available (M). If money is insufficient, consume as long as MU > P.

A consumer maximises total satisfaction from one product when the marginal utility of the last unit equals its price. If MU > P, buying more raises net benefit; if MU < P, the last unit cost more than it delivered — cut back. For a free good (P = 0), consume until MU = 0 — the point of maximum total utility.

Utility Maximization Across Two or More Goods

The Utility-Maximizing (Equi-Marginal) Rule The consumer’s money income should be allocated so that the last dollar (rupee) spent on each product yields the same amount of extra (marginal) utility:
MUA / PA = MUB / PB   (= MU of the last rupee, for every good bought)
As long as one good provides more utility per rupee than another, the consumer buys more of that good; its MU then diminishes until utility-per-rupee is equalised. When two or more goods are purchased this is called the equi-proportional utility rule.

Utility Maximization and the Demand Curve

When the price of product X falls, the quantity demanded of X increases. Before the fall, the consumer has a utility-maximizing combination of X with other goods — this gives the quantity demanded of X at the original price. After the price falls, a new utility-maximizing combination contains more X — a larger quantity demanded at the lower price. Tracing these utility-maximizing bundles at every price traces out a downward-sloping demand curve. Also remember: rising price → lower quantity demanded → higher marginal utility at the margin — restoring the equality between price and marginal utility.

Consumer Surplus Revisited

Consumer surplus is the difference between what a consumer is willing to pay for an additional unit of a good and the market price actually paid. The area between the demand curve and the price line measures total consumer surplus — willingness to pay comes straight from marginal utility.

Income and Substitution Effects

Income effect

The impact a price change has on a consumer’s real income / purchasing power. A price fall makes you effectively richer, so you buy more (for normal goods).

Substitution effect

The impact a price change has on a product’s relative expensiveness. A good that becomes relatively cheaper pulls purchases away from its alternatives.

When price falls, both effects normally push quantity demanded up — the second reason (after diminishing MU) that demand curves slope downward.

Basics of Macroeconomics

Lesson 07 · Chathuranga Adhikari

What is Macroeconomics?

Macroeconomics (from the Greek prefix makro- meaning “large”) deals with the total or aggregate level of output, consumption, investment, employment and the general price level in the economy. It is the study of the behaviour of the economy as a whole — the performance, structure, behaviour and decision-making of an economy as a whole rather than individual markets, including national, regional and global economies. It is also known as the theory of income and employment, or simply income analysis.

The three major concerns of macroeconomics 1. Unemployment · 2. Inflation · 3. Output growth

1. Unemployment

Unemployment refers to the situation where the population of a country does not find work to earn their livelihood — the ratio of the labour force that fails to get employment. The unemployment rate is a key indicator of the economy’s health; the existence of unemployment seems to imply that the aggregate labour market is not in equilibrium.

Labour force = the sum of employed + unemployed workers. The working-age population splits into people in the labour force and people not in the labour force.
To be unemployed, a person must want to work and be actively looking for a job (but not have found one).
Unemployment rate = (Number of unemployed ÷ Labour force) × 100Labour force = employed + unemployed (actively seeking work)

Why unemployment is a problem

  • Lost incomes and production — output that could have been made is gone forever
  • Lost human capital — prolonged unemployment permanently damages job prospects; benefits don’t fully replace wages
  • Consumption falls; people pay for necessities rather than luxuries (income-elastic goods suffer)
  • Quality of life of the unemployed worker declines

Effects on the economy & society

  • Reduced national output and lower economic growth potential
  • Lower standard of living — GDP falls, per-capita income falls, poverty rises
  • Reduced national income — less income tax and company profit tax collected; national development slows
  • Government spending rises on benefit payments while tax revenue falls
  • Social costs: unsafe family conditions, higher crime, stress and loss of confidence
  • With unemployment the economy operates inside its PPF — resources unused
Full employmentFull employment is the situation where the unemployment rate equals the natural unemployment rate — there is no cyclical unemployment; all unemployment is frictional and structural. It does NOT mean zero unemployment.

2. Inflation

Inflation is the rise in the general level of prices. When the general price level rises, each unit of currency buys fewer goods and services — inflation results in a loss of the value of money. A popular description: “too much money chasing too few goods”. In the long term, inflation erodes consumer purchasing power — accumulated wealth buys less and less. High inflation makes business planning difficult because raw-material costs are uncertain.

TermMeaning
InflationAn increase in the overall price level
Creeping inflationPrices rise very slowly “like a snail or creeper” — a moderate rate (around 3–5% or less) over a long period
HyperinflationA period of very rapid increases in the overall price level — a rare phenomenon
DeflationA decrease in the overall price level (negative inflation); prolonged deflation can be just as damaging as sustained inflation

Demand-pull inflation

Occurs when households, businesses, governments and foreign buyers collectively try to purchase more output than the economy can produce — excess demand pulls the price level up (“too much spending”).

Cost-push inflation

Inflation attributable to decreases in supply, primarily due to increases in production costs (wages, raw materials, energy) — costs push prices up.

Causes of hyperinflation

  • High money supply growth → demand rises with constant supply → prices rise
  • Sudden demand surges (demand-pull episodes)
  • Loss of confidence — citizens lose faith in the currency
  • War-like situations between countries

Effects of hyperinflation

  • Currency value depreciates; people flee into gold, real estate, commodities
  • Foreign investors back out; banking and financial institutions are disrupted
  • Shortage of basic food supplies
  • Exports cheaper, imports costlier; unemployment rises; government revenue falls

Causes of deflation

  • A fall in aggregate demand (AD shifts left)
  • A rightward shift of aggregate supply — lower production costs through improved technology

Why inflation & deflation are problems

  • They affect the distribution of income and wealth — wage contracts mean employees lose when inflation is unexpectedly high (wages do not rise immediately)
  • They can lower real GDP and employment

3. Output and Growth

Growth refers to a change in the level of economic activity from one year to another — poor and developing countries wish to attain rises in national income and per-capita income. Aggregate output is the total quantity of goods and services produced in an economy in a given period — the main measure of how well an economy is doing. Economists study what level of investment growth delivers the desired income and employment without inflation or deflation, achieving full utilisation of resources. Full employment maximises output with existing resources; growth increases output beyond them.

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

The monetary value of all finished goods and services produced within a country’s borders in a specific period (usually annual). Includes all private and public consumption, government outlays, investments, and exports minus imports. One of the primary indicators used to gauge the health of an economy.

National Income

The total value of all goods and services produced within a nation over a specified period — the sum of wages, profits, rents, interest and pension payments to residents. It gives a correct picture of the economy and the purchasing power of the people.

Economic growth = increase in the market value / productive capacity of goods and services produced by an economy over timeMeasured as the % change in real GDP between periods

Nature, Scope and Importance of Macroeconomics

Scope: macroeconomics studies aggregates or averages covering the entire economy — total employment, national income, national output, total investment, total consumption, total savings, aggregate supply, aggregate demand, general price level, wage level and cost structure. It is concerned with unemployment, economic fluctuations, inflation/deflation, international trade and economic growth.

Why macroeconomics matters

  • Helps us understand the functioning of a complicated modern economic system — how national income and employment are determined by AD and AS
  • Helps achieve economic growth — a higher GDP level and higher employment — and explains how to reach and sustain the highest state of growth
  • Helps bring price stability — analyses business fluctuations and suggests measures to control inflation and deflation
  • Explains the determinants of the balance of payments, identifies causes of deficits and suggests remedies
  • Solves big problems — poverty, unemployment, inflation, deflation — solvable only at the whole-economy level
  • Enables correct economic policy formulation and coordination of international economic policies
  • Has saved us from applying micro theory to problems that require an economy-wide view

International Trade

Course outline (Week 14–15) · supported by Lesson 07 exchange-rate content

Why Nations Trade

International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries. It gives rise to a world economy in which prices, supply and demand are affected by global events. Trade allows expansion of markets for goods and services that might otherwise be unavailable — it is the reason you can choose between a Japanese, German or American car. Greater competition brings more competitive prices and cheaper products home to the consumer. The theoretical foundations taught in this course are absolute advantage and comparative advantage.

Absolute Advantage

DefinitionA country has an absolute advantage in producing a good if it can produce more of it with the same resources (or the same output with fewer resources) than another country. Trade based on absolute advantage means each country specialises in what it produces best overall.

Comparative Advantage

DefinitionA country has a comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost than another country. This is the deeper concept — even a country worse at everything (no absolute advantage in anything) still gains from trade by specialising where its opportunity cost is lowest.
Opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = (units of Y given up) ÷ (units of X produced)Compare opportunity costs across countries — the country with the lower OC of X specialises in X
✏️ Worked example Absolute & comparative advantage

Sri Lanka and Thailand can each produce rice and clothing with 100 labour hours:

Rice (tons)Clothing (units)
Sri Lanka50100
Thailand80120

(i) Who has an absolute advantage in each good? (ii) Compute opportunity costs and find the comparative advantage. (iii) What should each country specialise in?

(i) Absolute advantage: Thailand produces more of both goods with the same hours — absolute advantage in rice AND clothing; Sri Lanka has none. (Yet trade still pays — watch.)

(ii) Opportunity costs:

  • Sri Lanka: 50 rice ⇔ 100 clothing. OC of 1 rice = 100/50 = 2 clothing; OC of 1 clothing = 50/100 = 0.5 rice.
  • Thailand: 80 rice ⇔ 120 clothing. OC of 1 rice = 120/80 = 1.5 clothing; OC of 1 clothing = 80/120 = 0.67 rice.

Comparative advantage: Sri Lanka produces rice more cheaply in foregone clothing (2 < 1.5? — no!). Careful: lower opportunity cost wins. Rice: Sri Lanka 2 clothing vs Thailand 1.5 clothing → Thailand has the lower OC of rice. Clothing: Sri Lanka 0.5 rice vs Thailand 0.67 rice → Sri Lanka has the lower OC of clothing.

(iii) Specialisation: Sri Lanka → clothing (0.5 rice per unit); Thailand → rice (1.5 clothing per ton). Any trade price between the two OC ratios (1.5 clothing per rice < world price < 2 clothing per rice) benefits both countries — each ends up consuming beyond its own PPC.

Exchange Rates and Trade

The exchange rate between two currencies is the rate at which one currency will be exchanged for another — the value of one country’s currency in terms of another. It governs the terms on which international trade and investment take place:

Nominal exchange rate

The rate at which monies of different countries can be exchanged for one another (e.g. USD/LKR). A depreciation makes exports cheaper to foreigners and imports costlier.

Real exchange rate

The rate at which goods and services produced in different countries can be exchanged for one another — nominal rate adjusted for price levels. It determines competitiveness.

Trade and the balance of paymentsAll trade transactions are recorded in the balance of payments (BOP) — the record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world in a period, made by individuals, firms and government bodies. A stable exchange rate is one of the goals of macroeconomic policy (Topic 7).
